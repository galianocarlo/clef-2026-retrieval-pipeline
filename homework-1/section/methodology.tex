\subsection{BM25 First-Stage Retrieval}
\label{sec:bm25}

The first retrieval stage uses BM25~\cite{Harman1994}, a probabilistic
ranking function that extends the BIM (Binary Independence Model)~\cite{RobertsonSparckJones1976} with two
components that BIM lacks: term-frequency (TF) saturation and document-length
normalisation.  The full ranking function is

\begin{equation}
R(q,d) \;\propto\;
  \sum_{i \,\in\, q \cap d}
  \underbrace{
    \log\frac{(r_i+0.5)\,(N-n_i-R+r_i+0.5)}
             {(n_i-r_i+0.5)\,(R-r_i+0.5)}
  }_{\text{IDF-like weight (BIM)}}
  \cdot
  \underbrace{
    \frac{(k_1+1)\,f_i}
         {k_1\!\left(1-b+b\,\tfrac{dl}{avdl}\right)+f_i}
  }_{\text{TF saturation}}
  \cdot
  \underbrace{
    \frac{(k_2+1)\,qf_i}{k_2+qf_i}
  }_{\text{query TF}}
\label{eq:bm25}
\end{equation}

\noindent
where $N$ is the number of documents in the collection; $n_i$ is the document
frequency of term $i$; $R$ and $r_i$ are relevance feedback counts (set to zero
in the absence of feedback); $f_i$ is the within-document frequency of term $i$;
$qf_i$ is its frequency in the query; $dl$ is the document length in tokens;
$avdl$ is the mean document length across the collection; and $k_1$, $b$, $k_2$
are free parameters.

\paragraph{TF saturation ($k_1$).}
The TF component is a sigmoid-like function of $f_i$ that saturates as $f_i$
grows, approaching $(k_1+1)/k_1$ asymptotically.  This models the intuition
that the first occurrence of a term is highly informative whereas the hundredth
adds negligible evidence.  At $k_1=0$ the function reduces to pure IDF; as
$k_1\!\to\!\infty$ it degenerates to raw, unsaturated TF.

\paragraph{Length normalisation ($b$).}
The factor $\bigl(1-b+b\,dl/avdl\bigr)$ penalises long documents, which
naturally accumulate higher raw term frequencies regardless of relevance.
Setting $b=0$ disables normalisation entirely; $b=1$ applies full
normalisation by dividing TF by the relative document length.

\paragraph{Query TF ($k_2$).}
For standard keyword queries where each term appears exactly once
($qf_i=1$), the query-TF component reduces to $1$ and has no effect.
It becomes relevant only when the query itself is derived from a document
containing repeated terms.


\subsection{V1: Lexical Baseline and Probabilistic Retrieval}
\label{subsec:methodology_v1}
Our foundational pipeline (V1) relied exclusively on exact keyword 
matching via Apache Lucene, utilizing the BM25 probabilistic scoring model. 
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Theoretical Foundation :}(This can be \textbf{replaced/integrated}
    with configuration) BM25 improves upon standard TF-IDF 
    by introducing term frequency saturation and document length normalization. 
    Given the dense nature of scientific and medical abstracts, we calibrated 
    the parameters to $k_1=0.9$ and $b=0.8$. This calibration strictly limits 
    the relevance of repeated acronyms, preventing long documents from being 
    penalized while avoiding term saturation.
    \item \textbf{Multilingual Indexing:} To preserve complex medical terminology
    and hyphenated compounds, we indexed documents using native language-specific analyzers
    (\texttt{EnglishAnalyzer}, \texttt{FrenchAnalyzer}, \texttt{GermanAnalyzer})
    rather than generic NLP tokenizers (we tried with a first tokenizer). 
    \item \textbf{The Bottleneck:} While this baseline performs well for English queries,
    where the vocabulary is standardized, it suffers from an important vocabulary mismatch
     for cross-lingual tasks (that includes French and German). The main issue of Lexical models
     is that they fail when query terms lack exact character-level translations in the target documents.
\end{itemize}

%this can be moved here:
\paragraph{Configuration.}
We set $k_1=0.9$ and $b=0.8$, departing slightly from the commonly
reported defaults of $1.2$ and $0.75$.  The lower $k_1$ increases TF
saturation speed, reducing the influence of high-frequency terms in
scientific abstracts (e.g.\ domain-specific verbs that recur heavily
but carry little discriminative power).  The higher $b$ strengthens
length normalisation, which is appropriate for a collection in which
document length varies considerably between short news-style abstracts
and full structured abstracts.  The query-TF parameter $k_2$ is not
exposed by Lucene's \texttt{BM25Similarity} implementation; the
component is effectively fixed at $k_2\!\to\!\infty$, giving raw
query TF, but since every query term appears at most once in our
setting this has no practical effect.

BM25 scoring is computed by Apache Lucene's \texttt{BM25Similarity},
which applies per-field language-specific analysers (English, French,
German, and a \texttt{StandardAnalyzer} fallback for other languages)
before indexing and query processing, ensuring that stemming and
stop-word removal operate in the correct morphological context for
each field.

\subsection{V2: Statistical Query Expansion via Pseudo-Relevance Feedback}
\label{subsec:methodology_v2}
To bridge the vocabulary gap without introducing external neural models, our second
iteration (V2) explored a statistical expansion technique known as \ac{PRF}.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mechanism:} PRF assumes that the initial top-$k$ retrieved documents are highly relevant. 
    The system retrieved the top 3 documents, merged their multilingual text fields, and returned the 5 most frequent terms,
    applying stopword removal techniques with its multilingual stopwords list.
    These terms were then appended to the original query as a \texttt{SHOULD} clause to proactively expand the search vocabulary.
    \item \textbf{Theoretical Limitation (Topic Drift):} While the concept behind this theory seemed very promising,
    empirical observation showed high susceptibility to topic drift. When the initial retrieval was marginally inaccurate,
    the statistically extracted terms shifted the semantic focus away from what the user had been looking for.
    Due to the high precision required in the medical domain, this purely statistical expansion proved to be unsuitable.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{V3: Asymmetric Search and Semantic Re-ranking}
\label{subsec:methodology_v3}
Once understood the performances of the statistical expansion, with V3 we introduced Deep Learning to understand the semantic intent behind the queries.
We adopted an asymmetric architecture: English queries were routed through the fast BM25 pipeline, while French and German queries went through a two-stage retrieve-and-rerank process.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Semantic Scoring:} Our experiment used a Transformer-based Cross-Encoder (\texttt{mmarco-mMiniLMv2-L12-H384-v1}). 
    Unlike Bi-Encoders, Cross-Encoders perform full self-attention across both the concatenated query and document, allowing the deep, word-level semantic interactions of importance in cross-lingual understanding.
    \item \textbf{Max-P Chunking Strategy:} A key theoretical constraint of Transformers is that they have a fixed-length context window (512 tokens for our selected model), 
    which causes information loss as not all of the relevant information contained in the medical papers is considered. 
    To bypass this, we implemented a Max-P (Max-Passage) sliding-window strategy, where
    documents were partitioned into independent chunks of 250 words, with a 50-word overlap to preserve semantics. The final relevance score for the document would be based on the highest-scoring chunk.
    \item \textbf{The Retrieval Bottleneck:} Although the Cross-Encoder performs well at re-ordering candidate documents,
    it was limited by BM25's initial retrieval suitability. If the lexical engine failed to retrieve a relevant cross-lingual document (based upon vocabulary mismatch), the neural re-ranker was unable to evaluate it.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{V4: Fully Asymmetric Hybrid Retrieval with Dense Vectors}
\label{subsec:methodology_v4}
To resolve the retrieval bottleneck identified in V3, our final architecture (V4) fully embraced Dense Retrieval, constructing a Hybrid candidate pool prior to the re-ranking phase.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Vector Space Projection:} To project queries and documents into a shared continuous
    vector space, we have integrated FAISS (Facebook AI Similarity Search) to avoid exact-match limitations. The corpus was encoded using the \texttt{intfloat/multilingual-e5-base} model.
    \item \textbf{Hybrid Candidate Pooling:} To create the starting candidate pool for French and German
    queries, the first step was to take the exact-keyword precision candidates from Lucene and the contextual candidates from FAISS. This dual-pool strategy maximized the initial Recall,
    ensuring relevant documents bypassed language barriers prior to being re-ranked by the Cross-Encoder.
    \item \textbf{Score Normalization and Tail Padding:} Combining two different types of scoring, BM25 and Cross-Encoder logits,
    presents an architectural challenge due to the large difference in their scoring ranges. To solve this issue,
    a unified ranking that preserves theoretical Recall@1000 without corrupting top-$k$ Precision, was accomplished using a tail-padding strategy.
    Documents retrieved by the neural re-ranker populated the top ranks, while the remaining unranked candidates retrieved by BM25 were appended to the tail with a discounted scores (computed as $-1000.0 - rank$). 
    This mathematically prevents evaluation frameworks from erroneously resorting the list, preserving the integrity of both the deep semantic precision at the top and the broad lexical recall at the bottom of the ranking.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Document and Query Parsing}

Documents and queries are parsed through dedicated classes that implement
the Python iterator protocol, allowing the system to process the files sequentially without loading the whole collection into memory in one step.



\subsubsection{Document Parsing}
The \texttt{JsonArrayDocumentParser} reads the data collection in sets of
500 documents. For each document, the title and abstract are concatenated together and passed to the \texttt{langdetect} library to assign a language label (\texttt{en}, \texttt{de}, \texttt{fr}, or \texttt{other}).
Each document in the collection is coded as a \texttt{ParsedDocument} object
carrying its ID, title, abstract, and detected language (which will be later used to determine the analyzer in the indexing phase).

\subsubsection{Query Parsing}
Queries are parsed by the \texttt{JsonArrayQueryParser}, which follows the
same pattern built for document parsing. Since being social media tweets and not scientific paper as in the case of the documents, queries are "cleaned":
mentions, URLs, and hashtag symbols are removed while alphanumeric content and essential punctuation are kept. 
Moreover, the \texttt{extract\_entities()} method scans each quary looking for the presence of scientific journal names from \texttt{journals.txt};
if the match has a positive correspondence, in searching phase documents published on that journal will get a boost.
An important difference to bring to light regarding the language detection: queries are partitioned for language, hence no language tag is assigned to them.


\subsection{Data Preprocessing}
The linguistic textual preprocessing, both for documents and queries, is executed through the Lucene's built-in analyzers:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{EnglishAnalyzer} — tokenization, lowercase conversion, English stopword removal, and Porter stemming
    \item \texttt{GermanAnalyzer} — tokenization, lowercase conversion, 
German stopword removal, and Snowball stemming
    \item \texttt{FrenchAnalyzer} — tokenization, lowercase conversion, 
French stopword removal, and French light stemming
    \item \texttt{StandardAnalyzer} — tokenization and lowercase 
    conversion only, used as a fallback for other languages with no query correspondence (Italian and Spanish)
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Regular Expressions}
The very first textual preprocessing applied was "manually" built since relied on:
\begin{itemize}
    \item lowercase conversion to ensure case-insensitive matching.
    \item regular expression for tokenization and HTML tag removal. In detail \texttt{re.sub(r'<[{\textasciicircum}]+>', '', text)} 
    to strip HTML tags and \texttt{RegexpTokenizer(r'[A-Za-z0-9\%\textbackslash.\textbackslash-]+')} 
    to extract only alphanumeric tokens, percentages, and hyphenated 
    compounds such as \textit{SARS-CoV-2}.
    \item the NLTK library for Snowball stemming and stopword removal
\end{itemize}

This approach was discarded because of two reasons, the first one related to the risk of mismatching between document and query indexing, and - more importantly - the run score 
obtained with the manual approach was less effective than those achieved using Lucene's analyzers.

An early preprocessing approach explored during development relied on 
a custom \texttt{TextProcessor} class built on top of NLTK. For each 
document or query, the pipeline applied HTML tag removal via regular 
expressions, tokenisation through a \texttt{RegexpTokenizer} 
(\texttt{[A-Za-z0-9\%\textbackslash.\textbackslash-]+}), stopword 
removal, and Snowball stemming — all handled explicitly in Python for 
English, French, and German.




\subsection{Embeddings}
As mentioned previously, our information retrieval pipeline built exploits embeddings. To clarify how the are used it is necessary to make a  distinction between \textbf{sparse} and \textbf{dense} embeddings. 
\textit{Sparse} embeddings are high-dimensional vectors in which the majority of elements are 0, making them computationally lightweight. These vectors do not catch any sentence meaning, and therefore they are suitable for exact-matching task. On the other hand, \textit{Dense} embeddings are low-dimensional vectors, where each dimension carries a fragment of semantic meaning. In detail, the contextual embeddings created with the model
paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM-L12-v2 are \textit{contextual}, allowing the retrieval of information that goes beyond the "vocabulary mismatch".
