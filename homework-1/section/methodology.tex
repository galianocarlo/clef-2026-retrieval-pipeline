\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}
We developed these pipelines based on the knowledge gained during the course.
This section describes the indexing and retrieval pipeline.  We first
present the BM25 ranking model that underpins every version
(\S\ref{sec:bm25}), then the document and query parsers
(\S\ref{subsec:parsing}), the linguistic preprocessing
(\S\ref{subsec:preprocessing}), and the indexing layer
(\S\ref{subsec:indexing}).  The four iterative versions
(V1\,–\,V4) are described in \S\ref{subsec:methodology_v1}\,–\,\S\ref{subsec:methodology_v4}.


% ======================================================================
\subsection{BM25}
\label{sec:bm25}

Every version uses BM25~\citep{Harman1994,RobertsonEtAl1994}, a
probabilistic ranking function that extends the Binary Independence
Model~\citep{RobertsonSparckJones1976} with two components that the BIM
lacks: \ac{TF} saturation and document-length normalisation.  The full
ranking function is

\begin{equation}
R(q,d) \;\propto\;
  \sum_{i \,\in\, q \cap d}
  \underbrace{
    \log\frac{(r_i+0.5)\,(N-n_i-R+r_i+0.5)}
             {(n_i-r_i+0.5)\,(R-r_i+0.5)}
  }_{\text{IDF-like weight (BIM)}}
  \cdot
  \underbrace{
    \frac{(k_1+1)\,f_i}
         {k_1\!\left(1-b+b\,\tfrac{dl}{avdl}\right)+f_i}
  }_{\text{TF saturation}}
  \cdot
  \underbrace{
    \frac{(k_2+1)\,qf_i}{k_2+qf_i}
  }_{\text{query TF}}
\label{eq:bm25}
\end{equation}

\noindent
where $N$ is the number of documents in the collection; $n_i$ is the
document frequency of term $i$; $R$ and $r_i$ are relevance-feedback
counts (zero in the absence of feedback); $f_i$ is the within-document
frequency of term $i$; $qf_i$ is its frequency in the query; $dl$ is
the document length in tokens; $avdl$ is the mean document length
across the collection; and $k_1$, $b$, $k_2$ are free parameters.

\paragraph{TF saturation ($k_1$).}
The TF component is a sigmoid-like function of $f_i$ that saturates as
$f_i$ grows, approaching $(k_1+1)/k_1$ asymptotically.  This models
the intuition that the first occurrence of a term is highly
informative whereas the hundredth adds negligible evidence.  At
$k_1=0$ the function reduces to pure IDF; as $k_1\!\to\!\infty$ it
degenerates to raw, unsaturated TF.

\paragraph{Length normalisation ($b$).}
The factor $\bigl(1-b+b\,dl/avdl\bigr)$ penalises long documents,
which naturally accumulate higher raw term frequencies regardless of
relevance.  Setting $b=0$ disables normalisation entirely; $b=1$
applies full normalisation by dividing TF by the relative document
length.

\paragraph{Query TF ($k_2$).}
For standard keyword queries where each term appears exactly once
($qf_i=1$), the query-TF component reduces to $1$ and has no effect.
It becomes relevant only when the query is itself derived from a
document containing repeated terms.

\subsection{Document and Query Parsing}
\label{subsec:parsing}

Documents and queries are parsed through dedicated classes that
implement the Python iterator protocol, so the system processes the
files sequentially without loading the whole collection into memory.

\subsubsection{Document Parsing}
The \texttt{JsonArrayDocumentParser} reads the corpus in batches of
$500$ JSONL records.  For each record, the title and abstract are
concatenated and passed to the \texttt{langdetect}
library~\citep{Shuyo2010} to obtain a language code.  Codes outside
$\{\texttt{en},\texttt{fr},\texttt{de}\}$, together with inputs that
raise \texttt{LangDetectException} on very short or noisy text, are
mapped to a single \texttt{other} bucket.  Determinism is enforced by
setting \texttt{DetectorFactory.seed = 0}, so repeated runs over the
same collection produce identical labels.  Each parsed record is
wrapped in a \texttt{ParsedDocument} carrying the document identifier,
title, abstract, and detected language; the language label is later
consumed by the indexer to route the document to the appropriate
per-field analyser (\S\ref{subsec:indexing}).

\subsubsection{Query Parsing}
Queries are parsed by \texttt{JsonArrayQueryParser}, mirroring the
batched-iterator pattern of the document parser.  Because the queries
are social-media tweets rather than scientific articles, a cleaning
step is applied before any retrieval logic: user mentions
(\texttt{@handle}) are stripped and the
hashtag character is removed but the term itself is preserved,
the leading numeric prefix of enumerated tweets (e.g.\ \texttt{1)\,},
\texttt{2/\,}) is dropped, and the Lucene-reserved tokens
\texttt{AND}/\texttt{OR}/\texttt{NOT} are lowercased so that an
upper-case sentence opener does not crash the query parser.
Alphanumeric characters and a small set of essential punctuation
(period, comma, hyphen, parentheses, percent sign) are kept; everything
else is removed.

Unlike documents, queries are not passed through \texttt{langdetect}: the dataset partitions tweets per language \textit{a priori}, and the language code is read directly from the file name (\texttt{en\_dev.jsonl}, \texttt{de\_dev.jsonl},
\texttt{fr\_dev.jsonl}).

\subsection{Linguistic Preprocessing}
\label{subsec:preprocessing}

All linguistic preprocessing, for both documents and queries, is
delegated to Apache Lucene analysers~\citep{McCandlessEtAl2010},
wrapped in a \texttt{PerFieldAnalyzerWrapper} so that each
(title/abstract, language) field receives its own analysis chain:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{\texttt{EnglishAnalyzer}} — Unicode tokenisation,
    lowercase conversion, English possessive stripping, English
    stopword removal, and Porter stemming~\citep{Porter1980}.
  \item \textbf{\texttt{FrenchAnalyzer}} — elision filtering
    (\textit{l'}, \textit{d'}, \ldots), lowercase conversion, French
    stopword removal, and the French light stemmer.
  \item \textbf{\texttt{GermanAnalyzer}} — lowercase conversion,
    German stopword removal, normalisation of umlauts and \textit{\ss},
    and the German light stemmer.
  \item \textbf{\texttt{StandardAnalyzer}} — Unicode-aware
    tokenisation, lowercase conversion, and removal of the default
    English stopword set; used as a typed-but-language-agnostic
    fallback for documents whose detected language is neither English,
    French, nor German (e.g.\ Italian or Spanish).
\end{itemize}

Using the same analyser at both index time and query time ensures that
identical surface forms map to identical indexed terms on both sides
of the inverted-index lookup, which is essential for stemmed languages
such as German where the surface form and the stem can differ
substantially.

\paragraph{Discarded alternative: a hand-rolled NLTK pipeline.}
An earlier preprocessing variant relied on a custom
\texttt{TextProcessor} class layered on top of
NLTK~\citep{BirdLoper2004}.  HTML tags were stripped with
\texttt{re.sub(r'<[\textasciicircum>]+>', '', text)}; tokens were
extracted with to retain alphanumerics, percentages and hyphenated compounds such as
\textit{SARS-CoV-2}; stopwords were removed with NLTK's per-language
lists; and stems were produced by NLTK's Snowball
implementation~\citep{Porter2001Snowball}.  We abandoned this pipeline
for two reasons.  First, executing the analysis in Python at index
time and again at query time made it difficult to guarantee
bit-identical token streams on the two sides, risking subtle
index/query mismatches.  Second, in pilot runs against the dev split
it was strictly less effective than the Lucene-analyser configuration
above, while introducing an additional external dependency.

\subsection{Indexing}
\label{subsec:indexing}

The collection is indexed by \texttt{DirectoryIndexer}, a Python class
that walks the corpus directory and feeds qualifying files through
\texttt{JsonArrayDocumentParser}.  A whitelist parameter
(\texttt{include\_files = \{collection.jsonl\}}) restricts the walk to
the document file proper, so the topic and query files which share
the same folder and \texttt{.jsonl} extension are skipped rather than
mis-indexed as documents.

\subsubsection{Field schema}
Each \texttt{ParsedDocument} is converted into a Lucene
\texttt{Document} carrying four fields:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{id} — \texttt{StringField} (not analysed, stored).
    Persists the original \texttt{pubkey} so retrieved hits can be
    written into the TREC run file.
  \item \texttt{lang} — \texttt{StringField} (not analysed, stored).
    Persists the language label assigned at parse time.
  \item \texttt{title\_\textlangle lang\textrangle} and
    \texttt{abstract\_\textlangle lang\textrangle} — typed text
    fields carrying the document body.  The language suffix routes
    each document to the analyser configured for its language; e.g.\
    a record labelled \texttt{de} populates only \texttt{title\_de}
    and \texttt{abstract\_de}, leaving the remaining language fields
    empty.  This per-language fan-out lets the English-, French-, and
    German-specific analysis chains coexist inside a single index.
\end{itemize}

The text fields share a singleton \texttt{FieldType} configured with
\texttt{DOCS\_AND\_FREQS\_AND\_POSITIONS}, tokenisation
enabled, and the original text stored.  Storing positions enlarges
the index by approximately $15\text{--}20\,\%$ but is required to
support phrase and proximity queries (\textit{``machine learning''});
storing the raw text supports stored-field retrieval in the
cross-encoder re-ranking stages of V3 and V4.

\subsubsection{Scoring and writer configuration}
The \texttt{IndexWriter} is configured with BM25 similarity
($k_1=0.9$, $b=0.8$; see \S\ref{sec:bm25}), a $256$-MB RAM buffer,
\texttt{OpenMode.CREATE} (so each indexing run starts from scratch),
\texttt{commitOnClose=true}, and compound files disabled to keep
segment inspection straightforward.  Indexing the $10{,}000$-document
collection completed in under one minute on the Docker-hosted CPU
configuration described in \S\ref{sec:setup}.

\subsubsection{Dense companion index}
\label{subsubsec:faiss-index}
For the hybrid pipelines (V3, V4) a second indexer in
\texttt{index\_faiss.py} runs alongside the Lucene indexer and
produces a FAISS-backed dense index~\citep{JohnsonDouzeJegou2021}
over the same collection.  Each title and abstract is concatenated
into a single \texttt{title.\,abstract} string, embedded by a
sentence-transformer encoder (\texttt{BAAI/bge-m3}~\citep{ChenEtAl2024BGEM3}
for V4, \texttt{intfloat/multilingual-e5-base}~\citep{WangEtAl2024E5}
for V3, configurable via the \texttt{FAISS\_MODEL} environment
variable), $\ell_2$-normalised, and added to a flat inner-product
index (\texttt{faiss.IndexFlatIP}).  Because the vectors are
$\ell_2$-normalised, inner-product search on this index is
mathematically equivalent to cosine-similarity search.  Two artefacts
are written next to the Lucene segments: \texttt{corpus.faiss} (the
serialised FAISS index) and \texttt{doc\_ids.json} (the parallel list
of document identifiers needed to map FAISS row indices back to
Lucene's \texttt{id} field).  Embedding is performed in batches of
four documents to bound peak GPU memory at the precision (FP16) and
dimensionality ($1024$ for bge-m3) used in V4.

\subsection{V1: Lexical Baseline and Probabilistic Retrieval}
\label{subsec:methodology_v1}

The foundational pipeline (V1, \autoref{fig:v1-pipeline}) relied
exclusively on exact keyword matching via Apache Lucene and the BM25
probabilistic scoring model.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{figure/02-figure_v1-diagram.png}
  \caption{V1 — full pipeline.  \emph{Top:} offline corpus path
    (responsible: \texttt{index.py}), \texttt{collection.jsonl}
    $\to$ \texttt{JsonArrayDocumentParser} (with deterministic
    \texttt{langdetect}) $\to$ \texttt{ParsedDocument} $\to$
    \texttt{DirectoryIndexer} (per-field analysers, BM25 scoring)
    $\to$ a single Lucene index.  \emph{Bottom:} online query path
    (responsible: \texttt{search.py}), tweet $\to$
    \texttt{JsonArrayQueryParser} $\to$ \texttt{ParsedQuery} $\to$
    tweet cleaning + the same per-field analyser $\to$ BM25 search.
    The Lucene index links the two halves of the pipeline.}
  \label{fig:v1-pipeline}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Configuration.}
We set $k_1=0.9$ and $b=0.8$, departing slightly from the commonly
reported defaults of $1.2$ and $0.75$.  The lower $k_1$ accelerates
TF saturation, reducing the influence of high-frequency terms in
scientific abstracts (e.g.\ domain-specific verbs that recur heavily
but carry little discriminative power).  The higher $b$ strengthens
length normalisation, which is appropriate for a collection in which
document length varies considerably between short news-style
abstracts and full structured abstracts.  The query-TF parameter
$k_2$ is not exposed by Lucene's \texttt{BM25Similarity}
implementation; the component is effectively fixed at
$k_2\!\to\!\infty$, giving raw query TF, but since every query term
appears at most once in our setting this has no practical effect.

\paragraph{Multilingual Indexing.}
To preserve complex medical terminology and hyphenated compounds, we
indexed documents using native language-specific analysers
(\texttt{EnglishAnalyzer}, \texttt{FrenchAnalyzer},
\texttt{GermanAnalyzer}) rather than a generic tokeniser; an early
NLTK-based variant was discarded for the reasons given in
\S\ref{subsec:preprocessing}.  BM25 scoring is computed by Apache
Lucene's \texttt{BM25Similarity}, which applies the per-field
language-specific analysers (English, French, German, and a
\texttt{StandardAnalyzer} fallback for other languages) before
indexing and query processing, ensuring that stemming and stopword
removal operate in the correct morphological context for each field.

\paragraph{The Bottleneck.}
While this baseline performs well for English queries, where the
vocabulary is standardised, it suffers from a substantial vocabulary
mismatch on cross-lingual tasks (French and German).  Lexical models
fail when query terms lack exact character-level translations in the
target documents, motivating the experiments that follow.

\subsection{V2: Statistical Query Expansion via Pseudo-Relevance Feedback}
\label{subsec:methodology_v2}

To bridge the vocabulary gap without introducing external neural
models, the second pipeline (V2, \autoref{fig:v2-pipeline}) explored
a statistical expansion technique known as Pseudo-Relevance
Feedback~\citep{LavrenkoCroft2001}.



\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{figure/03-figure_v2-diagram.png}
  \caption{V2 — full pipeline.  Same offline indexing as V1; the
    online query path adds an intermediate BM25 pass over the top-3
    documents to extract the five most frequent multilingual content
    words, which are appended as a \texttt{SHOULD} clause to the
    original query before a second BM25 pass.}
  \label{fig:v2-pipeline}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Mechanism.}
PRF assumes that the initial top-$k$ retrieved documents are highly
relevant.  The system retrieves the top-3 documents from a first
BM25 pass, merges their multilingual title and abstract fields, and
selects the five most frequent content terms after stopword removal
against a curated multilingual list.  These terms are then appended
to the original query as a \texttt{SHOULD} clause to expand the
search vocabulary while the original query is kept as a
\texttt{MUST} constraint.

\paragraph{Theoretical Limitation.}
As discussed in \S\ref{sec:results}, the technique is highly
susceptible to topic drift.  When the initial retrieval is even
marginally inaccurate, the statistically extracted terms shift the
semantic focus away from the user's intent.  Given the high precision
required in the medical domain, this purely statistical expansion
proved unsuitable.

\subsection{V3: Hybrid Asymmetric Search and Semantic Re-ranking}
\label{subsec:methodology_v3}

Drawing on the limits of the previous pipelines, the third
experiment (V3, \autoref{fig:v3-pipeline}) moved from statistical
expansion to a deep-learning approach intended to capture semantic
similarity through embeddings.  We adopted an asymmetric pipeline
that combines high-precision re-ranking with computational
efficiency.



\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.99\linewidth]{figure/04-figure_v3-diagram.png}
  \caption{V3 — full pipeline.  Two parallel \texttt{DirectoryIndexer}
    runs over the same \texttt{ParsedDocument} stream produce a
    Lucene inverted index and a FAISS dense index (\texttt{e5-base},
    $\ell_2$-normalised) sharing identifiers via
    \texttt{doc\_ids.json}.  English queries follow a fast BM25-only
    path; French and German queries fuse BM25 and dense candidates,
    chunk long abstracts to fit the 512-token cross-encoder, and
    aggregate per document via Max-P.  The BM25 tail pads the
    positions beyond the rerank pool.}
  \label{fig:v3-pipeline}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Hybrid Candidate Pooling.}
To resolve the retrieval bottleneck identified in
\S\ref{subsec:methodology_v1} — relevant cross-lingual documents
that BM25 fails to retrieve — we implemented dense retrieval over
FAISS~\citep{JohnsonDouzeJegou2021}.  For French and German queries,
the system merges BM25 candidates with those from a dense search
that uses the \texttt{intfloat/multilingual-e5-base}
encoder~\citep{WangEtAl2024E5}, recovering documents that share
meaning with the query but have no surface-vocabulary overlap.

\paragraph{Neural Re-ranking (Cross-Encoder).}
Scoring on the merged candidate pool is performed by a
Transformer-based cross-encoder
(\texttt{cross-encoder/mmarco-mMiniLMv2-L12-H384-v1})~\citep{BonifacioEtAl2021mMARCO,
NogueiraCho2019monoBERT}.  Unlike the bi-encoder used for first-stage
dense retrieval, which encodes query and document independently, the
cross-encoder concatenates them and applies full self-attention,
allowing deep word-level semantic interactions that are critical for
cross-lingual understanding.

\paragraph{Max-P Chunking Strategy.}
To respect the $512$-token context limit of this cross-encoder while
preserving the information in longer abstracts, we adopted a
sliding-window strategy.  Documents are partitioned into chunks of
$250$ words with a $50$-word overlap; each chunk is scored
independently, and the document's final score is the maximum across
its chunks (\textit{Max-P} aggregation~\citep{DaiCallan2019DeeperText}).

\paragraph{Asymmetric Optimisation.}
Given the existing strength of the lexical engine on English, English
queries bypass the neural pipeline and follow a fast BM25 path.  This
preserves computational budget for the harder French and German
cross-lingual tasks.

\paragraph{Tail Padding Integrity.}
To preserve the standard Recall@1000, documents retrieved by BM25 but
not selected for re-ranking are appended to the final results.  These
tail documents receive penalised scores using the formula
$\mathit{score} = -1000 - \mathit{rank}$, which prevents the
score-based sort used by evaluation toolkits from promoting tail
entries above the cross-encoder's reranked head.

\subsection{V4: Architecture with Long-Context Embeddings}
\label{subsec:methodology_v4}

Although V3 successfully addressed the cross-lingual vocabulary gap,
the chunking strategy added engineering complexity and risked
breaking long-range semantic dependencies.  The final iteration
(V4, \autoref{fig:v4-pipeline}) optimised the hybrid pipeline by
upgrading both neural components to long-context, retrieval-tuned
state-of-the-art models.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{figure/04-figure_v4-diagram.png}
  \caption{V4 — full pipeline.  Indexing layout matches V3, but the
    FAISS side now stores $1024$-dimensional bge-m3 embeddings.  At
    query time the chunking and Max-P stages of V3 are removed: the
    upgraded cross-encoder \texttt{bge-reranker-v2-m3} accepts up to
    $1024$ tokens and accommodates nearly all abstracts in a single
    forward pass.}
  \label{fig:v4-pipeline}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Long-Context Bi-Encoder (BGE-M3).}
We replaced the dense encoder with
\texttt{BAAI/bge-m3}~\citep{ChenEtAl2024BGEM3}, a retrieval-tuned
multilingual model with a context window of up to $8192$ tokens.
The window is far larger than any abstract in the collection, so
each document is now encoded in a single pass on the FAISS side,
removing both information loss and the architectural complexity of
the sliding-window mechanism.

\paragraph{Long-Context Cross-Encoder (BGE-Reranker-v2-m3).}
The cross-encoder was upgraded to
\\ \texttt{BAAI/bge-reranker-v2-m3}~\citep{ChenEtAl2024BGEM3}, which
accepts up to $1024$ tokens per (query, document) pair, twice the
budget of the V3 reranker.  Because nearly all abstracts fit within
$1024$ reranker tokens, V4 removes the chunking and Max-P stages of
V3 entirely.  The chunking removal is therefore driven by the
\emph{cross-encoder} upgrade, not by the bi-encoder's $8192$-token
window, which only governs first-stage dense retrieval.

\paragraph{Unified High-Dimensional Space.}
The dense embedding dimensionality grew from $768$ (e5-base) to
$1024$ (bge-m3), giving the first dense-retrieval stage a richer
semantic representation and a higher-quality candidate pool for the
re-ranker on French and German queries.

\paragraph{Refined Hybrid Integrity.}
Asymmetric routing is preserved in V4 along with the tail-padding
strategy introduced in V3.  The combination of long-context dense
retrieval, exact-match lexical backup, and protected evaluation
integrity constitutes the most robust configuration we evaluated for
cross-lingual scientific-claim retrieval.

\subsection{Embeddings}
\label{subsec:embeddings}

The retrieval pipelines exploit two complementary families of
embeddings.  \emph{Sparse} embeddings are high-dimensional vectors
in which the majority of entries are zero — concretely, the
inverted-index posting lists exposed by Lucene.  They are
computationally lightweight and excel at exact-match retrieval, but
do not encode sentence-level meaning.  \emph{Dense} embeddings are
low-dimensional, contextual vectors in which each dimension carries
a fragment of semantic meaning, computed by transformer encoders
trained for retrieval (\texttt{multilingual-e5-base} in V3,
\texttt{bge-m3} in V4).  Because dense vectors place semantically
similar documents close together regardless of surface form, they
recover relevant documents that lexical retrieval misses, narrowing
the cross-lingual vocabulary gap that motivates V3 and V4.
